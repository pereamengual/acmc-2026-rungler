The approach

What happens when a system’s behaviour arises from its own feedback rather than from a sequence imposed from outside?

The workshop builds the Rungler and the Blippoo Box one idea at a time. Each stage adds a single new element to the previous one, so that you can hear and see what that element changes: first noise, then a clock and a memory, then a shift register, feedback, a comparator, a resonator, and finally two Runglers coupled to each other.

Every stage has live monitors (oscilloscope, spectrum, XY, event map, the register itself) and a block diagram whose blocks link to the code that runs them.

Learning path · six stages

  1. 0a

    Continuous noise

    Noise as raw material: heard directly, through a filter, and slowed down to move the pitch of a sine oscillator. No clock, no memory.

    STOCHASTIC
  2. 0b

    Clocked sample & hold

    A clock samples the noise and holds each value as a pitch; a quantizer snaps it to octaves, a major scale or every semitone.

    CLOCKED
  3. 1

    The core Rungler

    In three steps: an 8-bit shift register takes the place of the sample & hold; an XOR loops its last bit back; its voltage feeds back into the two oscillators.

    RECURSIVE
  4. 2

    PWM by comparator

    A comparator turns the two oscillators’ triangles into a pulse whose width keeps changing — the Rungler draws the timbre.

    COMPARATOR
  5. 3

    Exciter & Twin Peak

    The pulse excites a Twin Peak resonator, two resonant filters subtracted, as in the Blippoo Box: each edge becomes a ping.

    RESONANCE
  6. 4

    Blippoo Box architecture

    In three steps: two cross-coupled Runglers; a sample & hold that warps time; and the same voltage spreading the two peaks apart.

    SYSTEM

Why plain JavaScript in an AudioWorklet

The instruments were first sketched in Cycling ’74 gen~ and exported to the browser with RNBO. For the workshop they are rewritten as readable JavaScript running in a standard AudioWorklet, so that participants can inspect, modify and preserve them, and see their internal state at audio rate.

  1. Preservation. The lab depends only on the W3C Web Audio API, not on a specific version of a vendor runtime. Preserving Hordijk’s ideas also means keeping the tools that carry them alive.
  2. Code as workshop material. Open a block’s code, change one line, and hear the result — without installing anything or owning a licence.
  3. Internal state at audio rate. The XY, register and event-map views read the bits, the DAC value and the phases straight from the engine.
  4. One model for research and teaching. The code follows the structure of the research prototype, so the two can be checked against each other. Here it is a didactic model, not a research result.
  5. Numerical decisions in plain sight. Every limiter, saturation or clock-edge interpolation is written out and can be discussed.
  6. Testable without sound. The engine also runs outside the browser, where it is checked by automated tests.
  7. Robust in the classroom. Small script files, nothing to compile or download; the whole lab also fits in a single file that works offline.
  8. Consistent with Resistance Music. The tool itself does not depend on a platform.

What this is — and credits

Didactic models. The stages are functional models of the instruments’ block diagrams, built for listening and understanding. They are not emulations of the circuits, and the monitors show the model, not measurements of hardware.

Rob Hordijk. The Rungler, the Benjolin and the Blippoo Box are the work of Rob Hordijk. The Twin Peak resonator and the Blippoo Box architecture follow his description in R. Hordijk, “The Blippoo Box: A Chaotic Electronic Music Instrument, Bent by Design,” Leonardo Music Journal 19 (2009): 35–43, doi:10.1162/lmj.2009.19.35.
